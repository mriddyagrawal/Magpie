Benchmark report · 2026-09-01 · companion to the POPE run

Prompt Ordering at Long Context

The POPE benchmark said prompt ordering doesn’t matter. It was right — for 156-token prompts. Re-run over documents up to 16k tokens, the same manipulation swings accuracy by 30 points, and the effect scales almost linearly with context length.

LFM2.5-VL-3B · Q6_K llama.cpp 10502 · 0adcc3bb5 · CUDA RTX 4090 LongBench HotpotQA · n=500 greedy · T=0 3000 generations · 35 min · ~$0.55

The headline

Putting the question before a long document costs 13.6 points of accuracy versus putting it after. p = 1.3×10−8.

And the effect is entirely a function of length. Below 4k tokens it is a clean null — 14 items flip one way, 15 the other. Above 8k tokens it is a 24-point gap with 70 of 82 discordant pairs falling the same direction. The POPE null was not wrong; it was measured in a regime where ordering has no room to matter.

The second finding is a negative one, and it only survived because the answers were read rather than string-matched: Magpie’s GBNF grammar has no measurable effect on accuracy at any ordering. It reliably fixes output format — but the apparent accuracy gain in the raw automatic scores was an artifact of grammar producing shorter answers, which token-F1 rewards regardless of correctness.

At short context the discordant pairs split 14 against 15 — about as perfect a null as a coin can produce. The entire effect lives in the long bin, where 70 of 82 disagreements go the same way.

Notice what STD does as documents grow. It falls from 0.650 to 0.255 — losing 40 points. SDT falls only from 0.658 to 0.494. Both degrade with length, as any model does; but question-first degrades roughly twice as fast. Long context does not merely dilute the question, it appears to strand it.

Reconciling the two experiments

POPE prompts were 156 tokens, of which 120 were the image and 8 the question. That sits far below even the 0–4k bin here — and the 0–4k bin is itself a null. The POPE result and this one are not in conflict; they are two points on the same curve. Ordering is free when everything fits comfortably in attention’s working range, and expensive when it does not.

That last row is why STD’s two grammar arms produce byte-identical answer fields: the grammar has nothing to correct, so it never intervenes.

The artifact that nearly became a finding

On raw token-F1 the grammar looked like a +3.6 point win at SDT. It isn’t. Grammar-off answers are verbose — “Based on the provided text, Brent Roger Wilkes was connected to the scandal involving Randy ‘Duke’ Cunningham” scores F1 0.07 against gold “Duke Cunningham”, while the grammar-constrained “Randy ‘Duke’ Cunningham” scores 0.80. Both are correct. Grammar-off had 27 such verbose-but-correct answers at SDT against grammar-on’s 9. Automatic scoring alone would have reported a grammar effect that is purely about answer length.

05How the answers were scored

Free-text answers cannot be string-matched honestly, and the verbosity asymmetry above is exactly why.

  • 1,072 clear hits (token-F1 ≥ 0.8) scored automatically correct.
  • 1,352 clear misses (F1 ≤ 0.2 and the gold answer absent as a substring) scored automatically wrong.
  • 576 escalated (19.2%) — everything ambiguous, plus 82 low-F1 answers that nonetheless contained the gold string. Those 82 would otherwise have been silently marked wrong, and 27 of them were in a single arm.

The escalated set was judged by six independent Sonnet 5 readers working from byte-identical instructions, on shuffled batches with the ordering and grammar labels stripped — so no judge ever saw a single condition, and judging noise could not correlate with an arm. 416 of 576 were judged correct. I then audited 14 verdicts by hand and agreed with all 14; two were borderline-lenient (accepting “tennis players” for gold “former tennis player”) but defensible.

What the design rules out

  • Content differences. Components tokenized independently and concatenated — identical token-ID multisets between STD and SDT, verified per item.
  • Sampling noise. Greedy argmax; no top-k, top-p or penalties in the chain. Every discordant pair is attributable to the prompt.
  • Batching non-determinism. Six independent worker processes, each single-stream with identical context and batch parameters. Separate processes do not share batching, so floating-point reduction order is fixed within every arm.
  • Scoring bias against verbosity. Corrected by the escalation rule and blind judging, as above.
  • Judge bias. Arm labels stripped, batches shuffled and arm-mixed, instructions byte-identical, verdicts sampled and audited.
  • Prompt tuning. One system prompt, identical across all six conditions, written before any results were seen.

What remains uncertain

  • Backend. Run on CUDA rather than the Metal build Mac users get. The chunk dump matched exactly and all arms shared the backend, so the comparison is unaffected — but absolute numbers could shift by a fraction of a point on Metal.
  • One dataset, one task shape. HotpotQA is multi-hop retrieval over Wikipedia paragraphs. Whether the same curve holds for summarisation or code is untested.
  • Length bins are confounded with difficulty. Longer HotpotQA contexts contain more distractor passages, so some of the degradation with length is the task getting harder, not just the question getting distant. The relative gap between orderings within each bin is still a fair comparison, but the curve’s steepness mixes both.
  • The STDT advantage is not robust. Significant with grammar off, null with grammar on. Treat it as suggestive.
  • Greedy only. Magpie ships at non-zero temperature; ordering effects under sampling are untested.

07What this means for Magpie

Put the question after the document. On documents past ~8k tokens this is worth roughly 24 accuracy points, which is a larger effect than most model upgrades would buy. It is also the arrangement that lets a KV prefix cache be reused across many questions about the same files — so the fastest-to-serve ordering and the most accurate ordering are the same one. There is no trade-off to manage.

Consider repeating the question before the document as well. STDT adds ~5 points over SDT with grammar off, at a cost of a few dozen tokens. The evidence is weaker — it did not replicate with grammar on — so this is worth an A/B in production rather than an unconditional change.

Keep the grammar, but not for accuracy. It buys nothing measurable in correctness. It does guarantee parseable output on the 19% of long-context responses where the model would otherwise drift into prose, and a response Magpie cannot parse is a failed answer regardless of whether the model knew the answer. That is reason enough.

The context-window ceiling is a quality question, not a resource one. KV cache measured at ~16 KB/token, and prefill time depends on tokens actually processed, not window allocated — so a wider window is nearly free in memory and time. What degrades is the answer: STD collapses from 0.650 to 0.255 as documents grow, and even the best ordering falls from 0.667 to 0.551. Whatever window you open, expect accuracy to fall with what you actually put in it.
